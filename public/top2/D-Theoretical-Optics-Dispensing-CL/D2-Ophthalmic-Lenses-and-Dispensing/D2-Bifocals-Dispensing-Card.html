Card05Bifocals

Two powers, one lens: segments and jump

A multifocal is a distance lens with a near add worked into it. What the exam wants is the segment types, why the image jumps at the line, and the fabrication and fitting words. Learn the shapes, the jump rule, and the bench vocabulary, and dispensing is recognition.

01 Segment types

By constructionBy shape / use
Fused: a higher-index button melted into a depression in the main lensRound (Kryptok): large image jump, narrow high reading zone
One-piece / solid: ground into one blank (Ultex, Executive)Flat-top / straight-top (D-seg): minimal jump, popular
Cemented: a wafer bonded onExecutive: full-width ledge; widest near, but thick, heavy, visible line
Split (Franklin): two half-lenses in one frameDouble-D trifocal: near up AND down (librarian, mechanic)

02 Image jump

At the top of the segment the eye suddenly enters plus power away from its optical center, so it hits base-down prism and the image jumps. The jump size is set by how far the segment optical center sits below the seg top.

Jump vs total displacement

Jump is only the segment's own prism at its top. Total displacement at the reading point also adds the distance lens's prism there. Choose a flat-top to minimize jump; the round segment (Kryptok) is the worst.

03 Trifocals and progressives

Trifocal

Adds an intermediate zone between distance and near. Its power is distance + half the add (a +2.50 add gives a +1.25 intermediate).

PAL (progressive)

No line: a corridor of increasing plus from distance to near, flanked by peripheral soft distortion. Needs a correct fitting height, set too low and the patient must lift the chin to reach the near zone. Prism thinning shaves the lower edge to cut thickness and weight. Engravings: the temporal circle carries the maker's logo, the nasal one the add.

04 At the bench and the frame

Fabrication words

Surfacing order: block, generate/grind, fine/smooth, polish, deblock (after layout marking). A semi-finished blank has a finished front, so you grind the back to power. Glazing = mounting the finished lens into the frame.

Fit and anisometropia

If a lens rides on the cheek, adjust that temple to lift the front. For unequal Rx, vertical imbalance at near is fixed with a slab-off (bicentric grind), and a mislaid optical center induces prism (Prentice) and asthenopia.

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Fused bifocal? A higher-index segment button melted into a depression in the main lens.
  2. Executive bifocal traits? Full-width segment: widest near field, but thick, heavy, visible line.
  3. Widest reading field, does not mind the line? Executive (Franklin-style) bifocal.
  4. What causes image jump? Base-down prism at the top of the segment (entering plus away from its optical center).
  5. Which segment jumps most vs least? Round (Kryptok) most; flat-top least (its OC is near the top).
  6. Occupational lens for near up and down (librarian)? Double-D trifocal.
  7. Trifocal intermediate power? Distance + half the near add.
  8. PAL fitting height too low? The patient lifts the chin to reach the near zone.
  9. Purpose of prism thinning in a PAL? Reduce lower-edge thickness and weight.
  10. Surfacing sequence? Block, generate/grind, fine/smooth, polish, deblock (after layout).
  11. Glazing vs semi-finished? Glazing = mounting the lens in the frame; semi-finished = front done, grind the back to power.
  12. Fix for vertical imbalance at near in anisometropia? A slab-off (bicentric grind).
