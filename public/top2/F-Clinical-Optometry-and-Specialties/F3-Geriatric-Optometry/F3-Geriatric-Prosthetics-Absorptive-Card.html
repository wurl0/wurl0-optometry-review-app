Card06Geriatric

The aging eye, and the lenses it needs

Age changes the whole visual system and stacks the odds toward four diseases. Adapt the exam, screen for the big four, and prescribe light, contrast, and protection. Then two specialty topics: the artificial eye and the tinted lens.

01 Normal aging, and adapting the exam

Expected changes: presbyopia, senile miosis (a smaller pupil needing more light), reduced contrast sensitivity, slower dark adaptation, a yellowing lens (less blue), dry eye, and arcus senilis.

Adapt at the chair

Give more light and higher contrast, go slower, use larger targets, and mind mobility and falls. A small senile pupil is exactly why the elderly read better in bright light.

02 The four that steal sight

The leading causes of vision loss in older adults, each with a signature pattern.

Hooks

Cataract is the leading treatable cause. AMD = central vision (reading, faces). Glaucoma = peripheral first (silent). Diabetic retinopathy = scattered vascular damage. Detail is on the Ocular Disease cards.

03 The whole patient

Geriatric care is holistic

Screen for systemic disease (diabetes, hypertension) and polypharmacy (drugs with ocular side effects), assess driving, falls, and cognition, and connect to low-vision rehab when correction is not enough. The eye exam is one window on general health.

04 Ocular prosthetics

After the eye is lost or shrunken

An anophthalmic socket (after enucleation or evisceration) is fitted with an ocular prosthesis, a custom acrylic artificial eye over an orbital implant, for cosmesis and lid support. A scleral shell is a thin prosthesis worn over a phthisical (shrunken) or disfigured but present eye. Both need handling and hygiene teaching.

05 Absorptive and tinted lenses

NeedChoice
Photophobia / glareGray or amber tint; Polaroid for reflected glare (avoid over-tinting)
UV / blue protectionUV-blocking (plastic absorbs more than glass), blue-light filters
Low-light contrastYellow / amber
ConveniencePhotochromic (darkens with UV)
Two facts to carry

Cobalt oxide tints glass blue; a glassblower/welder cataract is infrared damage (wear IR-absorbing lenses). Optical density measures how dark a tint is: 50% transmission is OD 0.30.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Normal aging ocular changes? Presbyopia, senile miosis, reduced contrast, slow dark adaptation, yellowing lens, dry eye, arcus.
  2. Why do the elderly read better in bright light? Senile miosis (small pupil) needs more light.
  3. Exam adaptations for older adults? More light and contrast, slower pace, larger targets, mind mobility/falls.
  4. The four leading age-related causes? Cataract, AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy.
  5. AMD vs glaucoma field pattern? AMD takes central vision; glaucoma takes peripheral first.
  6. Leading treatable cause? Cataract.
  7. What else does geriatric care screen? Systemic disease, polypharmacy, driving, falls, cognition; refer to low-vision rehab.
  8. Ocular prosthesis vs scleral shell? Prosthesis = artificial eye for an anophthalmic socket; scleral shell = thin prosthesis over a phthisical/present eye.
  9. Enucleation vs evisceration? Enucleation removes the whole globe; evisceration removes contents, keeps the scleral shell.
  10. Tint for photophobia/glare? Gray or amber; Polaroid for reflected glare (avoid over-tinting).
  11. Best UV absorber, glass or plastic? Plastic absorbs more UV than glass.
  12. Cobalt and glassblower cataract? Cobalt oxide = blue glass; glassblower/welder cataract = infrared damage.
