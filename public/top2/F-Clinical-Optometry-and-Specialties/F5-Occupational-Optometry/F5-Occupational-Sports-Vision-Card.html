Card05Occ & sports

Match the eyewear to the task and the hazard

Occupational and sports vision start with the same question: what does this person do, at what distance, with what risk? Then you correct for the working distance, protect against the hazard, and, for athletes, train the visual skills. Learn the ergonomics, the safety standards, and the tints.

01 Analyze the task

Map the working distances, gaze angles, and environment before prescribing. Standard multifocals assume desk-and-floor; occupational tasks often need intermediate and overhead zones.

Occupational multifocals

A double-D trifocal gives near BOTH at the bottom and the top, for a mechanic under a car, a librarian at high shelves, or an electrician. Match the segment layout to where the near work actually is.

02 Computer vision and ergonomics

Computer vision syndrome (digital eye strain)

Eyestrain, blur, headache, and a dry, gritty feeling from a reduced blink rate at the screen, plus neck and shoulder ache. Manage with the 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), ergonomics, an intermediate/computer correction, blink hygiene and lubricants, and AR/anti-glare.

03 Protective and safety eyewear

Impact first

Polycarbonate (or Trivex) is the impact material, mandatory for monocular patients and hazardous work. Industrial safety lenses meet ANSI Z87 (impact-rated), often with side shields or a wraparound. Welding needs filtered shades that block infrared and ultraviolet (a glassblower/welder cataract is IR damage).

The rule

One good eye plus any risk equals polycarbonate, always. Match the filter to the radiation (UV, IR, laser, welding).

04 Sports vision

Performance skills are trainable

Dynamic visual acuity, reaction time, eye-hand coordination, peripheral awareness, and tracking can be trained in athletes. Contact lenses (daily disposables) usually beat spectacles on the field.

Protection

Spectacles and contact lenses are not protective. Use polycarbonate sports goggles to an impact standard (ASTM). Basketball, racquet sports, and baseball are high eye-injury sports.

Match the tint to the sport

Yellow/amber: contrast in low light, fog, haze (dawn, indoor). Gray: neutral bright sun. Brown: contrast in bright sun. Polarized: glare off water and roads (fishing, driving).

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. First step in occupational prescribing? Analyze the task: working distances, gaze angles, and hazards.
  2. Double-D trifocal use? Near work both below AND above (mechanic, librarian, electrician).
  3. Computer vision syndrome symptoms? Eyestrain, blur, headache, dry gritty eyes, neck/shoulder ache.
  4. Why do screens dry the eyes? Reduced blink rate during concentrated screen work.
  5. The 20-20-20 rule? Every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
  6. Monitor placement? About arm's length (50 to 70 cm), top at or just below eye level (slight downgaze).
  7. Safety-eyewear material and rule? Polycarbonate (or Trivex); one good eye plus any risk = polycarbonate always.
  8. Industrial impact standard? ANSI Z87.
  9. Welding filter blocks? Infrared and ultraviolet (shaded filters).
  10. Sports eye protection? Polycarbonate goggles to an ASTM standard; spectacles/CL do not protect.
  11. Best lens modality for most sports? Daily disposable contact lenses.
  12. Tints: yellow vs polarized? Yellow/amber for contrast in low light/fog; polarized for glare off water and roads.
