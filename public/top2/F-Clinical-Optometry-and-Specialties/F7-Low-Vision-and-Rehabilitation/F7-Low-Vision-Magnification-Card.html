Card01Low vision

Make the image bigger, by the numbers

Low vision is what best correction cannot fix, so you enlarge the retinal image instead. Three routes make it bigger, and one short calculation says how much and with what lens. Learn the definitions, the four magnifications, and the calc chain, and the biggest block of this subject is yours.

01 The vocabulary and the numbers

The World Health Organization ladder: an ocular disorder leads to impairment (measurable loss of function), then disability (change in skills), then handicap (social disadvantage).

Term / cutoffMeaning
Impairment / disability / handicapFunction loss / skill change / social disadvantage
Severe impairmentVA worse than 6/60 down to 3/60
BlindnessVA worse than 3/60 (20/400), or field ≤ 10° from fixation
Leading cause of impairmentUncorrected refractive error (cataract leads blindness and geriatric low vision)

02 Four ways to enlarge the image

TypeHow
Relative sizeMake the object physically bigger (large-print)
Relative distanceBring the object closer (shorter working distance)
AngularAn optical system enlarges the apparent angle (magnifier, telescope)
Projection / electronicProject an enlarged image (CCTV / video magnifier)

03 The magnification calculation

The chain the exam loves: needed magnification, then its dioptric power, then the working distance.

The three formulas

Needed magnification = starting acuity / goal (20/200 to 20/40 = 200/40 = 5X). Dioptric power = 4 × M (25 cm reference), so 5X = +20 D. Focal distance = 100 / F cm, so +20 D focuses at 5 cm. A 20/100 target at 5X reads as 20/20.

Kestenbaum's shortcut

The near add to read 1M (newsprint) is roughly the reciprocal of the distance VA fraction: 20/200 needs about 200/20 = +10 D. A fast first estimate of the magnification a patient needs.

04 Devices, and matching them to the task

Near vs distance

High-add spectacles and hand/stand magnifiers for near reading (short working distance is the trade). Telescopes (hand-held or bioptic) for distance, signs and faces. Electronic / CCTV gives large, adjustable magnification and contrast, and its magnification multiplies with moving closer.

Non-magnifying help

Do not forget lighting, contrast, and glare control (tints, typoscopes, bold-line paper). For an eccentric-viewing patient, training the preferred retinal locus matters as much as the lens.

05 Cross-links

Say these out loud

Theoretical Optics: magnifier power and focal distance are F = 1/f and M = F/4. Primary Care (Presbyopia): the high-add and working-distance logic is the same. Ocular Disease / Geriatrics: cataract, AMD, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy are the conditions that fill the low-vision clinic.

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Impairment vs disability vs handicap? Measurable function loss / change in skills / social disadvantage.
  2. WHO blindness cutoffs? VA worse than 3/60 (20/400), or field 10 degrees or less from fixation.
  3. Severe impairment band? VA worse than 6/60 down to 3/60.
  4. Leading cause of impairment vs of geriatric low vision? Uncorrected refractive error (impairment); cataract (geriatric low vision and blindness).
  5. The four magnifications? Relative size, relative distance, angular, and projection/electronic.
  6. Needed magnification? Starting acuity / goal (e.g., 200/40 = 5X).
  7. Dioptric power of a magnifier? 4 x magnification (25 cm reference), so 5X = +20 D.
  8. Focal / working distance of +20 D? 100/F = 5 cm.
  9. A 20/100 target at 5X reads as? 20/20 (100/5).
  10. Kestenbaum's rule? Add for 1M print is about the reciprocal of the distance VA (20/200 gives ~+10 D).
  11. Telescope vs magnifier? Telescope for distance (signs, faces); magnifier/high add for near reading.
  12. A non-magnifying low-vision aid? Lighting, contrast, glare control (tints, typoscope), or PRL training.
