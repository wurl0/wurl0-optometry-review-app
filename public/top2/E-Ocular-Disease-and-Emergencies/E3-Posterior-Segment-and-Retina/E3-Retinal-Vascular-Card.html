Card01Retinal vascular

Retinal vascular disease: the five that get mixed up

This is the cluster that cost the most marks last time. Artery versus vein, hypertensive versus arteriosclerotic, diabetic, and central serous all sit on one fundus and blur together. Learn each by its one signature and the confusion goes away.

01 Artery vs vein: two very different fundi

An artery occlusion starves the retina pale. A vein occlusion floods it with blood. Everything else follows from that.

Why the pictures split

CRAO starves the inner retina, which turns white, while the fovea stays red on its intact choroidal supply, the cherry-red spot. It is a retinal stroke: sudden, painless, severe monocular loss with an RAPD. CRVO backs blood up behind the block, giving dilated tortuous veins and diffuse flame-and-blot hemorrhage, and weeks later a risk of neovascular glaucoma.

03 Hypertensive vs arteriosclerotic (the pair that trips you)

Acute pressure vs chronic wall

Hypertensive is the acute blood-pressure picture: arteriolar narrowing, flame hemorrhages, cotton wool spots, a macular star, and retinal edema giving a sheen. Arteriosclerotic is the chronic vessel-wall picture: copper then silver wiring, AV nicking, and vascular sheathing with visible lipid in the wall. If the stem says sheen and exudates, it is hypertensive; if it says wiring and sheathing, it is arteriosclerotic.

04 Diabetic retinopathy

The microaneurysm is the tell

The earliest and defining sign is the microaneurysm, with dot-and-blot hemorrhages and hard exudates. Neovascularization marks the jump to proliferative disease, with its risk of vitreous hemorrhage and tractional detachment. If a stem mentions microaneurysms or new vessels, it is diabetic, not hypertensive.

Staging in one line

NPDR: microaneurysms, dot-blot hemorrhage, hard exudates, venous beading. PDR: neovascularization, vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment. Macular edema is the main threat to central vision and can occur at any stage.

05 Central serous, and retinitis pigmentosa

CSCR

A focal RPE leak lets choroidal fluid seep under the macula, lifting a serous neurosensory detachment. The classic patient is a stressed young man or a steroid user, with a hyperopic shift, metamorphopsia, and micropsia. That description, macular edema from an RPE defect letting choroidal fluid in, is central serous chorioretinopathy.

Retinitis pigmentosa

Bone-spicule pigment in the mid-periphery, waxy disc pallor, and attenuated vessels. It is a rod-cone dystrophy: night blindness first, then a ring scotoma and tunnel vision, with an extinguished ERG.

06 Cross-links

Say these out loud

Visual Biology Card 2: the cherry-red spot is the retina's dual blood supply, the inner retina pale while the choroid keeps the fovea red. Ocular Pharma Card 4: the neovascular glaucoma after a CRVO or PDR is the drug story on the pressure side. Ocular Pharma Card 6: hydroxychloroquine and the systemic-disease drugs sit near this fundus. Primary Eye Care: ophthalmoscopy, the Amsler grid for CSCR, and the ERG grade these diseases.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Cherry-red spot? CRAO (central retinal artery occlusion).
  2. Blood-and-thunder fundus? CRVO (central retinal vein occlusion).
  3. Microaneurysms and dot-blot hemorrhage? Diabetic retinopathy.
  4. AV nicking with copper or silver wiring? Hypertensive / arteriosclerotic retinopathy.
  5. Macular star of hard exudate? Hypertensive retinopathy.
  6. Hypertensive vs arteriosclerotic, the split? Hypertensive = acute (narrowing, flame heme, cotton wool, macular star, edema/sheen); arteriosclerotic = chronic wall (copper/silver wiring, AV nicking, sheathing).
  7. Serous macular detachment in a stressed young man? Central serous chorioretinopathy.
  8. Bone-spicule pigment, waxy disc, night blindness? Retinitis pigmentosa.
  9. Why the cherry-red spot in CRAO? The pale ischemic inner retina surrounds a fovea still perfused by the choroid.
  10. Main late risk after CRVO or PDR? Neovascularization / neovascular glaucoma.
  11. Earliest diabetic sign, and what defines proliferative? Microaneurysm; neovascularization defines PDR.
  12. CRAO vs CRVO onset and color? CRAO sudden with a pale/white retina; CRVO more gradual and hemorrhagic.
