Card06Emergencies

Ocular emergencies: the first move wins the eye

These are the ones where minutes matter or the wrong first step costs vision. Two reflexes carry most of it: irrigate a chemical burn before you do anything else, and treat any sudden painless loss over fifty as arteritis until proven otherwise. This closes the Ocular Disease set.

01 The one you treat before you look

Chemical burn: irrigate first

A caustic splash is the one emergency where you irrigate immediately, before the history or the acuity, with copious fluid until the pH is neutral (around 7). Only then do you examine. Alkali (lye, ammonia, lime, cement) is worse than acid: it dissolves membranes and penetrates deep by liquefactive necrosis, while acid causes a self-limiting coagulative burn. Grade the severity by limbal blanching, the more white, ischemic limbus, the worse the outlook.

02 Sudden painless vision loss

CRAO

A retinal stroke: sudden, painless, severe monocular loss with a pale retina, a cherry-red spot, and an RAPD (Card 1). Minutes count, ocular massage, lower the IOP (paracentesis, acetazolamide), and always look for giant cell arteritis.

Over 50: think giant cell arteritis

An older patient with jaw claudication, scalp or temporal-artery tenderness, headache, or polymyalgia, and a high ESR/CRP. It causes arteritic AION, sudden painless loss with a pallid, chalky disc swelling, and can take the fellow eye within days. Start high-dose steroids immediately, do not wait for the biopsy. Every sudden painless loss over fifty gets an ESR and CRP.

03 Trauma

A soft, distorted eye after trauma is an open globe until proven otherwise, and the pupil often points to the wound.

Open globe: do not press

Signs: a peaked pupil, shallow anterior chamber, hyphema, low IOP, prolapsed uvea, positive Seidel, and a high-velocity history. Do not manipulate or press on the eye. Place a rigid shield (not a pad), keep the patient NPO, get a CT, give IV antibiotics and tetanus, and refer for urgent surgery.

Hyphema

Blood in the anterior chamber. Rest with the head up, a shield, and no aspirin or NSAIDs. Watch for the rebleed at day 3 to 5, an IOP spike, and corneal staining, and take extra care in sickle cell disease.

04 Endophthalmitis

A hypopyon after surgery

Intraocular infection, classically days 1 to 7 after cataract surgery or after an injection: pain, dropping vision, a hypopyon, and vitritis. This is an emergency, a vitreous tap plus intravitreal antibiotics (vancomycin and ceftazidime). The usual post-cataract organism is Staph epidermidis.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. First action in a chemical burn? Immediate copious irrigation, before history or acuity, until the pH is neutral.
  2. Alkali vs acid burns, which is worse and why? Alkali (liquefactive necrosis, penetrates deep); acid causes a self-limiting coagulative burn.
  3. CRAO management? Minutes count: ocular massage, lower the IOP, and check for giant cell arteritis.
  4. Sudden painless loss over 50, what to do? Suspect giant cell arteritis; check ESR and CRP and start high-dose steroids immediately, before the biopsy.
  5. Arteritic AION disc appearance? Pallid, chalky disc swelling.
  6. Open globe signs? Peaked pupil, shallow AC, hyphema, low IOP, uveal prolapse, positive Seidel.
  7. Open globe: what not to do, and what to do? Do not press or manipulate; place a rigid shield, keep NPO, CT, IV antibiotics, urgent surgery.
  8. Hyphema risks? Rebleed (day 3 to 5), raised IOP, corneal staining; caution in sickle cell; avoid aspirin and NSAIDs.
  9. Endophthalmitis triad and timing? Pain, dropping vision, hypopyon with vitritis; classically days 1 to 7 after cataract surgery.
  10. Endophthalmitis treatment? Vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics (vancomycin + ceftazidime).
  11. Most common post-cataract endophthalmitis organism? Staph epidermidis (coagulase-negative staph).
  12. Acute angle-closure, first move then definitive? Lower IOP fast, then laser peripheral iridotomy.
