The red eye: benign or blinding

A red eye is only a problem if it threatens sight. The whole skill is sorting the harmless conjunctivitis from keratitis, uveitis, angle-closure, and scleritis, and two clues do most of it: where the redness sits and what the pupil is doing.

01 Read the redness first

The pattern of injection sorts the causes before you touch anything. Diffuse and superficial is benign; a ring hugging the cornea is deep and dangerous.

Follicles vs papillae

Follicles (smooth lymphoid bumps) point to viral or chlamydial. Papillae (bumps with a central vessel) point to bacterial or allergic. And itch is the allergy tell, if it does not itch, it is probably not allergic.

04 The sight-threatening pair: keratitis and uveitis

Keratitis, by the clue

An infiltrate with an epithelial defect. Contact lens and fast melt with a hypopyon means Pseudomonas; a dendrite means HSV (antiviral only, no steroid on the epithelial form); feathery margins after plant trauma means fungus; pain out of proportion with a ring means Acanthamoeba. The drug picks are Ocular Pharma Card 2.

Anterior uveitis

Pain, photophobia, ciliary flush, a miotic pupil, and cells and flare in the anterior chamber, with keratic precipitates, sometimes a hypopyon, and posterior synechiae. Granulomatous (mutton-fat KPs, iris nodules) points to TB, sarcoid, syphilis, or VKH; non-granulomatous (fine KPs, acute, fibrinous) points to HLA-B27 disease (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, IBD).

The complication that raises pressure

Inflammation glues the iris to the lens as posterior synechiae, which block the pupil, bow the iris forward, and shut the angle, a secondary angle-closure glaucoma. Uveitis also brings cataract, cystoid macular edema, and band keratopathy.

05 Episcleritis vs scleritis

One drop tells them apart

Episcleritis is sectoral, mild or painless, self-limited, and blanches with phenylephrine 2.5%. Scleritis is a severe, boring, deep pain that wakes the patient, with a violaceous hue, does not blanch, and travels with systemic autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis). Necrotizing scleritis threatens the eye. The phenylephrine blanch is the separator.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Conjunctival injection vs ciliary flush? Conjunctival = superficial, diffuse, blanches with phenylephrine (conjunctivitis, episcleritis); ciliary flush = deep perilimbal, does not blanch (keratitis, uveitis, angle-closure, scleritis).
  2. Pupil in uveitis vs acute angle-closure? Uveitis miotic; acute angle-closure mid-dilated and fixed.
  3. Bacterial conjunctivitis: common bug, CL bug, hyperacute? Staph aureus; Pseudomonas (contact lens); Neisseria gonorrhoeae (hyperacute, emergency).
  4. Follicles vs papillae? Follicles = viral or chlamydial; papillae = bacterial or allergic.
  5. Hallmark of allergic conjunctivitis? Itching (bilateral, stringy mucus, chemosis).
  6. HSV epithelial keratitis sign and drug rule? Dendrite with terminal end-bulbs; antiviral only, no steroid on the epithelial form.
  7. Anterior uveitis signs? Pain, photophobia, ciliary flush, miosis, cells and flare, keratic precipitates, hypopyon, posterior synechiae.
  8. Granulomatous vs non-granulomatous KPs and causes? Granulomatous = mutton-fat KPs + iris nodules (TB, sarcoid, syphilis, VKH); non-granulomatous = fine KPs, acute (HLA-B27: AS, reactive arthritis, IBD).
  9. Uveitis complication that raises IOP? Posterior synechiae causing pupillary block and secondary angle-closure glaucoma.
  10. Episcleritis vs scleritis pain and phenylephrine? Episcleritis mild and blanches; scleritis severe, boring, violaceous, does not blanch.
  11. Scleritis systemic associations? Rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis.
  12. Trachoma significance? Chlamydial; the leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide.
