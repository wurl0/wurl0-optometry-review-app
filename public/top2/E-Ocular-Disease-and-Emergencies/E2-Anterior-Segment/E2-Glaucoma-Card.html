Card03Glaucoma

Glaucoma: the nerve, not just the number

Glaucoma is optic nerve damage with disc cupping and field loss. Pressure is a risk factor, not the diagnosis. Sort it by the angle, open or closed, and by primary or secondary, and the whole subject lines up. This is the disease behind the Pharma glaucoma card.

01 What it does to the disc

The damage shows at the optic nerve head. The cup enlarges and the neuroretinal rim thins, sometimes with a notch or a splinter hemorrhage.

Read the rim and the field

A healthy rim follows the ISNT rule, thickest Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal; break that order and suspect glaucoma. The field loss follows the nerve-fiber arcs: an arcuate (Bjerrum) scotoma and a nasal step first, sparing central vision until late, then tunnel vision. The ultimate effect of sustained pressure is this disc cupping.

Two labels the board reuses

POAG is the chronic simple or compensated glaucoma: open angle, no preexisting disease, aqueous reaching the angle at all times. If the stem says the aqueous cannot reach the trabecular meshwork because the angle is abnormal, that is angle-closure.

The synechiae trap

Posterior synechiae from uveitis cause secondary ANGLE-CLOSURE, not open-angle: the iris sticks to the lens, blocks the pupil, bows forward, and shuts the angle. And neovascular glaucoma is angle-closure too, from a rubeotic membrane dragging the angle shut.

04 Congenital and developmental

Primary congenital glaucoma

A big eye (buphthalmos), a hazy cornea with Haab striae, and the classic triad of epiphora, photophobia, and blepharospasm, from trabeculodysgenesis. Treatment is surgical (goniotomy or trabeculotomy). It also rides with Sturge-Weber, aniridia, Axenfeld-Rieger, Peters, and Marfan. Note the board favorite: Sturge-Weber does cause it, diabetes does not.

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. What defines glaucoma, and the role of IOP? Progressive optic neuropathy with disc cupping and field loss; IOP is a risk factor, not the definition.
  2. POAG features? Open angle, insidious, painless, peripheral field loss first, bilateral and asymmetric; the compensated / chronic simple glaucoma.
  3. Acute angle-closure features and mechanism? Sudden severe pain, halos, nausea, mid-dilated fixed pupil, hazy cornea, very high IOP; pupillary block shuts the angle.
  4. Definitive treatment of angle-closure? Laser peripheral iridotomy (after lowering IOP fast).
  5. At-risk profiles, angle-closure vs open-angle? Angle-closure: hyperopes, older, Asian, dim light. Open-angle: age, myopia, family history, African ancestry.
  6. Disc changes in glaucoma? Enlarged cup, thin or notched neuroretinal rim, splinter hemorrhage, raised or asymmetric cup-to-disc ratio.
  7. The ISNT rule? A healthy rim is thickest Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal; violation is suspicious.
  8. Glaucomatous field defects, in order? Arcuate (Bjerrum) scotoma and nasal step first, central spared, tunnel vision late.
  9. Two secondary open-angle causes? Pigment dispersion and pseudoexfoliation (also steroid-induced, angle recession).
  10. Neovascular glaucoma arises from? Rubeosis iridis after proliferative diabetic retinopathy or a CRVO.
  11. How does uveitis cause secondary glaucoma? Posterior synechiae and pupillary block, giving secondary angle-closure.
  12. Primary congenital glaucoma triad, and one non-cause? Epiphora, photophobia, blepharospasm (with buphthalmos); diabetes does not cause it, but Sturge-Weber does.
