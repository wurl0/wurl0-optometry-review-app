Card05Adnexa & blood

Lids, tears, and blood: the front and the plumbing

The tear film is the meeting point of the whole front of the eye: oil from the lids, water from the lacrimal gland, mucus from the conjunctiva. Learn that three-way handshake, follow a tear out to the nose, and trace the one artery that feeds it all.

01 The tear film: three layers, three sources

Three thin layers keep the cornea smooth and fed. Each comes from a different part of the adnexa, and the board wants the layer-to-gland pairing.

One immunology fact the board reuses

The aqueous layer carries the surface's defenses, and the most abundant immunoglobulin in tears is secretory IgA, backed by lysozyme and lactoferrin.

02 The eyelids

Front to back the lid runs skin, orbicularis, orbital septum, tarsal plate, the openers, then conjunctiva. Two muscles do the work, and the board pairs each with its nerve and its palsy.

The lid lumps

Chalazion: chronic, painless lipogranuloma of a blocked meibomian gland. Internal hordeolum: acute meibomian infection. External hordeolum (stye): acute infection of the glands of Zeis or Moll at a lash. Chronic and central to the lid is a chalazion; acute and at the margin is a stye.

03 The conjunctiva

Three parts, one key cell

It runs palpebral (lining the lids) to the fornix to bulbar (over the sclera to the limbus). Its goblet cells make the mucin layer and are densest in the fornix. And its vessels tell you depth: conjunctival injection is superficial, mobile, and blanches with phenylephrine (conjunctivitis), while ciliary (limbal) injection is deep and points to keratitis or uveitis.

04 The lacrimal system

Make the tears

The main lacrimal gland (superotemporal) drives reflex tearing on parasympathetic CN VII. The accessory glands of Krause and Wolfring give the steady basal secretion.

Drain the tears

A tear leaves by the pathway below and ends in the nose. A block anywhere gives epiphora (overflow) or a dacryocystitis (infected sac); in a baby, an imperforate valve of Hasner is the classic congenital blockage.puncta → canaliculi → common canaliculus → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → inferior meatus (valve of Hasner)

05 The blood supply

One artery feeds the eye

The ophthalmic artery is the first branch of the internal carotid, entering through the optic canal. Its central retinal artery feeds the inner retina, the short posterior ciliary arteries feed the choroid and the optic nerve head, and the long posterior and anterior ciliary arteries supply the anterior segment.

Why a stye can be dangerous

The superior and inferior ophthalmic veins are valveless and drain backward into the cavernous sinus. That open road is why infection from the mid-face and orbit can spread inward to a cavernous sinus thrombosis. The vortex veins, separately, drain the choroid.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Tear film three layers and their sources? Lipid (meibomian glands), aqueous (lacrimal glands), mucin (conjunctival goblet cells).
  2. Which gland makes the lipid layer and what does it prevent? Meibomian glands; it prevents evaporation.
  3. Which cells make the mucin layer? Conjunctival goblet cells (densest in the fornix).
  4. Lid muscles and their nerves? Orbicularis (CN VII, closes), levator (CN III, opens), Muller's (sympathetic, ~2 mm).
  5. Chalazion vs hordeolum? Chalazion = chronic painless meibomian lipogranuloma; hordeolum = acute infection (internal meibomian, external Zeis/Moll).
  6. Tear drainage pathway? Puncta, canaliculi, common canaliculus, lacrimal sac, nasolacrimal duct, inferior meatus (valve of Hasner).
  7. Site of congenital nasolacrimal obstruction? An imperforate valve of Hasner.
  8. Main lacrimal gland innervation vs accessory role? Parasympathetic CN VII for reflex tears; accessory Krause and Wolfring glands give basal secretion.
  9. Most abundant immunoglobulin in tears? Secretory IgA.
  10. The ophthalmic artery branches from? The internal carotid artery (its first branch), via the optic canal.
  11. Why are the ophthalmic veins clinically dangerous? They are valveless and drain to the cavernous sinus, so infection can spread backward.
  12. Which arteries feed the choroid and optic nerve head? The short posterior ciliary arteries.
