Card01The pathway

The visual pathway: where the lesion sits is the answer

Field-defect questions are pure pattern recognition. Learn the six relay stations and the one crossing rule, and every stem collapses to a location. Then two rules, congruity and macular sparing, settle the ties. This is the backbone of Visual Biology.

01 The relay, and the one crossing

Six stations carry sight from eye to brain: retina, optic nerve, chiasm, optic tract, LGN, optic radiation, visual cortex. The only trick is the chiasm, where the nasal fibers cross and the temporal fibers stay. From the tract back, each side of the brain owns the opposite half of the world.

04 The two rules that break ties

Congruity: posterior = matching

The more posterior the lesion, the more congruous the two eyes' defects look. An optic-tract hemianopia is incongruous (the two halves do not match); an occipital one is perfectly congruous. So a very symmetric homonymous defect points to the back of the brain.

Macular sparing = occipital

The occipital pole that maps the macula has a dual blood supply (middle and posterior cerebral arteries), so a posterior-cerebral stroke often spares central vision. A homonymous hemianopia that keeps the very center is occipital until proven otherwise.

Bitemporal hook

The chiasm carries the crossing nasal fibers, and nasal retina sees the temporal field. Press up on it with a pituitary tumor and both temporal fields drop out, bitemporal hemianopia. Tunnel toward the temples.

05 The pupil light reflex takes a detour

The reflex uses the same front end but branches off before the LGN, which is why it survives cortical blindness and why one eye's light moves both pupils.

Afferent arc

Light → retina → optic nerve → chiasm/tract → pretectal nucleus in the midbrain (not the LGN) → both Edinger-Westphal nuclei → CN3 out to the ciliary ganglion and iris sphincter. Because the pretectum feeds both sides, shining light in one eye constricts both pupils (direct and consensual).

RAPD

A relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn) is an optic nerve or large retinal problem. On the swinging-flashlight test, the affected eye's pupils dilate as the light swings to it. A cataract or refractive error does not cause an RAPD. The efferent motor arm of this reflex is the pupil pharmacology from Ocular Pharma Card 1.

06 Cross-links

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Order the pathway stations? Retina, optic nerve, chiasm, optic tract, LGN, optic radiation, occipital cortex (V1).
  2. What crosses at the chiasm? Nasal retinal fibers (which carry the temporal visual fields).
  3. Optic nerve lesion defect? Ipsilateral monocular vision loss.
  4. Chiasm (pituitary) defect? Bitemporal hemianopia.
  5. Optic tract defect? Contralateral incongruous homonymous hemianopia.
  6. Meyer's loop defect? Contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky").
  7. Parietal radiation defect? Contralateral inferior quadrantanopia.
  8. Occipital cortex defect and hallmark? Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.
  9. Congruity rule? The more posterior the lesion, the more congruous the defect.
  10. Why macular sparing in occipital strokes? Dual blood supply of the occipital pole (middle + posterior cerebral arteries).
  11. Pupil light reflex afferent relay, and the key difference from vision? Retina, optic nerve, then the pretectal nucleus (not the LGN), then both Edinger-Westphal nuclei, then CN3.
  12. An RAPD localizes to? The optic nerve or extensive retinal disease (not a cataract or refractive error).
