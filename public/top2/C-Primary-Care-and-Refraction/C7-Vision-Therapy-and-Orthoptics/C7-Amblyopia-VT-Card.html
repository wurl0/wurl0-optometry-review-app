Card06Amblyopia & VT

Train the lazy eye, rebuild the team

Amblyopia is reduced acuity in a structurally normal eye, the brain turned it down. Fix it in order: give a clear image, force the weak eye to work, then rebuild the two-eye team. Learn the types, the management ladder, and the therapy stages, and the vision-therapy questions line up.

01 What amblyopia is

Reduced best-corrected acuity (or a two-line difference between eyes) with a normal eye exam, from disrupted vision during the critical period of development. Depth perception (stereopsis) suffers first, and the earlier and longer the disruption, the deeper the loss.

The catch

A child reading 20/80 in one eye and 20/20 in the other with healthy-looking eyes is amblyopic until proven otherwise. Treat earlier for a better result, but plasticity persists longer than once thought.

02 The types

TypeCause
StrabismicA constant, unilateral turn; the deviating eye is suppressed

MeridionalUncorrected high astigmatism in one meridian

03 The management ladder

Work the steps in order. The first clinical goal is a clear retinal image, everything else builds on it.

The moves, spelled out

Step 1: full refractive correction gives the amblyopic eye a clear image to work with. Step 2: occlusion (patch the dominant/better eye) or atropine penalization blurs the good eye to force the weak one. Step 3: active therapy builds monocular skills. Step 4: anti-suppression and fusion training rebuild binocular vision.

04 Passive vs active, and the therapy stages

Passive vs active

Passive: occlusion and penalization just force the eye to be used. Active: supervised, feedback-driven tasks (Brock string, aperture-rule, monocular-in-a-binocular-field). The gains come from neuroplasticity and visual learning, not stronger muscles.

The stages of vision therapy

Monocular (each eye alone) → MFBF (monocular fixation in a binocular field, one eye works while both stay open) → biocular → binocular (both eyes as one system, fusion and stereopsis). Anti-suppression bridges from monocular into binocular.

A couple of named tools

CAM stimulator (rotating high-contrast gratings for the amblyopic eye) and pleoptics (dazzle the periphery, protect the fovea to recentre fixation). Success = improved acuity, no suppression, and restored stereopsis.

05 The functional anomalies (recap)

The accommodative and vergence dysfunctions you correct at the chair. The AC/A and phoria detail is on Physiological Optics Card 6; here is the clinical shorthand.

AnomalyClinical picture
Accommodative insufficiencyNear blur, receded near point, high lag; struggles to focus near
Ill-sustained / infacilityFades with sustained near work / slow to shift focus near-to-far (fails flippers)
Accommodative excess (spasm)Over-focuses, a lead, pseudomyopia, hard to relax to distance
Convergence insufficiencyExo at near, receded NPC, words jump, covers one eye at near
Convergence excessEso at near, high AC/A; helped by a plus add at near
Comitant vs incomitant

Comitant: the deviation is the same in every gaze (constant angle). Incomitant: it changes with gaze direction (a nerve palsy or a restriction).

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Amblyopia? Reduced best-corrected acuity with a structurally normal eye, from disrupted vision in the critical period.
  2. Strabismic amblyopia? From a constant unilateral turn; the deviating eye is suppressed.
  3. Anisometropic amblyopia? From a large unequal refractive error chronically blurring one eye.
  4. Deprivation amblyopia? Something blocks the image (congenital cataract, ptosis); the most severe.
  5. First step in management? Correct the refractive error to give a clear retinal image.
  6. Which eye do you patch? The dominant/better eye, to force the amblyopic one.
  7. Passive vs active treatment? Passive = occlusion/penalization (forced use); active = supervised feedback tasks (Brock string, aperture rule).
  8. What is MFBF? Monocular fixation in a binocular field: one eye works while both stay open (breaks suppression).
  9. Vision therapy stages? Monocular to MFBF to biocular to binocular (fusion and stereopsis).
  10. Mechanism of VT gains? Neuroplasticity and visual learning, not muscle strengthening.
  11. Accommodative insufficiency vs excess? Insufficiency = near blur, receded NPA, high lag; excess = over-focuses (lead, pseudomyopia, hard to relax).
  12. Convergence insufficiency signs? Exo at near, receded NPC, words jump, covers one eye at near.
