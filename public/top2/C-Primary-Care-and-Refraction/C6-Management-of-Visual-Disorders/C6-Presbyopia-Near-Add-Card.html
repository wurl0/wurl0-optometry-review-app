Card04Presbyopia

The lens runs out of plus to give

Presbyopia is not disease, it is arithmetic: the near task asks for more focusing than the aging lens can supply, so you make up the shortfall with an add. Learn the amplitude-demand logic, the half-amplitude reserve, and the multifocal choices, and every add question is a subtraction.

01 What presbyopia is

With age the crystalline lens stiffens and loses elasticity, so accommodative amplitude falls. Around 40 to 45 it drops below the near demand and near vision blurs.

The picture

Near blur, holding print farther away, needing more light, and eyestrain after sustained near work. Distance acuity is untouched, presbyopia is a near-only problem.

Two exam traps

The cause is decreased lens elasticity (not increased). And presbyopia never reduces distance vision, if distance is blurred too, look for another error.

02 Where it begins: amplitude vs demand

03 Finding the add

Demand minus what the eye can still spare

Measure the accommodative demand (1 / working distance, so 40 cm = 2.50 D) and the patient's amplitude. Keep half the amplitude in reserve and use the other half: Add = demand − ½ amplitude.

Worked example

Works at 40 cm (demand 2.50 D), amplitude 2.00 D. Keep 1.00 in reserve, use 1.00: Add = 2.50 − 1.00 = +1.50 D.

Refine and balance

Confirm with the near cross cylinder and balance with NRA/PRA: the ideal add sits at the midpoint, tentative add + (NRA + PRA)/2. Then prescribe the weakest add that gives comfortable, sustained near vision.

04 Working distance drives everything

Measure it first

The add only serves one distance. Establish the patient's habitual working distance before prescribing, because it sets the demand. An accountant at 40 cm and a musician reading a score at 60 cm need different adds.

Reading the complaint

Blur closer than the set distance = the add is too weak (or the task moved nearer). Must hold material farther out = the add is too strong or working distance was overestimated.

05 Delivering the add: multifocals

OptionNote
Single-vision readersSimplest; only near, must be removed for distance
Bifocal (flat-top, round, executive)Distance up, near add below; visible line, image jump at the segment
TrifocalAdds an intermediate = distance + half the near add
PAL (progressive)No line; a corridor of increasing plus; needs correct fitting height
Add limits and the intermediate

A near add rarely exceeds about +2.50 to +3.00 D; beyond that, the working distance is so close it needs a low-vision magnifier. The trifocal intermediate is half the add, so a +2.50 add gives a +1.25 intermediate (total = distance + 1.25).

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. What is presbyopia and when does it start? Age-related loss of accommodative amplitude from a stiffening lens; near blur around 40 to 45.
  2. The mechanism? Decreased elasticity of the crystalline lens (not the muscles).
  3. Does presbyopia affect distance vision? No, it is a near-only problem.
  4. Add formula? Add = accommodative demand minus half the amplitude (keep half in reserve).
  5. Demand at 40 cm? 2.50 D (1 / 0.40 m).
  6. Compute: 40 cm, amplitude 2.00 D? Add = 2.50 - 1.00 = +1.50 D.
  7. Balancing the add with NRA/PRA? Ideal add = tentative + (NRA + PRA)/2; they should be roughly equal and opposite.
  8. Why measure working distance first? It sets the accommodative demand, and the add serves only that distance.
  9. Near add too strong vs too weak? Too strong = must hold material farther; too weak = blur when the task is closer.
  10. Trifocal intermediate power? Distance + half the near add.
  11. Practical maximum add? About +2.50 to +3.00 D; beyond that use low-vision magnification.
  12. Goal when choosing the final add? The weakest plus that gives comfortable, sustained near vision.
