The autonomic eye: pupil, focus, and pressure

Almost every drop that dilates, constricts, focuses, or drops the pressure is just pulling one of four autonomic levers on two muscles. Learn the wiring once and mydriatics, cycloplegics, miotics, glaucoma drugs, and the pupil tests stop being a list to memorize.

01 Start with the wiring, not the drugs

Two muscles in the iris pull the pupil in opposite directions, and one muscle behind them bends the lens for near. Each runs on one branch of the autonomic nervous system, through one receptor. That is the whole map.

Why the ciliary muscle matters twice

When the ciliary muscle contracts it slackens the zonules, so the lens rounds up for near focus. The same contraction tugs on the scleral spur and pulls the trabecular meshwork open, so aqueous drains faster and pressure drops. That second job is exactly why pilocarpine, an accommodation drug, is also a glaucoma drug.

Hook

Para = pupil small (both start with P). Sympathetic does the opposite: big pupil, no near focus. If you only remember one thing, remember that the parasympathetic runs both the sphincter and the ciliary muscle, so blocking it dilates AND kills accommodation at the same time.

The classic flip

Phenylephrine dilates but does not cycloplege. It works on the α1 dilator, so it never touches the ciliary muscle and near focus is preserved. Antimuscarinics do both, because the parasympathetic runs both muscles. If a stem says "dilated, still can accommodate," think sympathomimetic, not atropinic.

Trap in the mnemonic

The common list "A Home So Cool Today" (Atropine, Homatropine, Scopolamine, Cyclopentolate, Tropicamide) is not in duration order. By duration, Scopolamine outlasts Homatropine (days vs a day or two). Anchor on the numbers above, not the rhyme.

High-yield pair

Atropine = strongest, longest cycloplegic. Tropicamide = weakest cycloplegic, best clean mydriatic. Cyclopentolate sits in the middle and is what you actually reach for in a cycloplegic refraction. Watch cyclopentolate in small children: it can cause CNS effects (drowsiness, ataxia, hallucinations).

Cataract surgery: which direction?

The pupil goes opposite ways at the two ends of the operation, and the board tests the reversal. Before cataract extraction you need the pupil wide for access, so you dilate with a mydriatic (tropicamide, often with phenylephrine). After the IOL is in, you constrict to trap and hold the implant and stop it displacing, so you use a miotic (carbachol or intracameral acetylcholine). Anchor it: open to get the lens out, close to keep the new lens in. Do not answer a constrictor for "before" or a dilator for "after."

04 Diagnostic pharmacology, one idea does the work

Nearly all the pupil drug tests run on denervation supersensitivity: cut the nerve to a muscle and its receptors multiply, so a dose too weak to move a normal pupil now moves the denervated one. Say it in plain terms first, then the tests name themselves.

Adie's tonic pupil (parasympathetic denervation)

Large pupil, poor light reaction, slow tonic near response. The ciliary ganglion is damaged, so the M3 receptors are supersensitive. Dilute pilocarpine 0.125% constricts an Adie's pupil but leaves a normal one alone. Constriction confirms it.

Horner's syndrome (sympathetic denervation)

Miosis, ptosis, anhidrosis. Two jobs, two drops. Confirm with cocaine: it blocks noradrenaline reuptake, so a normal pupil dilates and a Horner's pupil does not (there is no noradrenaline being released to pool). Apraclonidine does the modern version in reverse: the supersensitive α1 dilator makes the Horner's pupil dilate and the lid lift. Localize with hydroxyamphetamine: it releases stored noradrenaline, so it dilates only if the last (postganglionic, third-order) neuron is intact. No dilation means the lesion is postganglionic.

Blown pupil: drops or nerve?

A fixed dilated pupil that will not constrict to pilocarpine 1% is pharmacologic (atropinic) blockade, the receptors are occupied. A third-nerve palsy pupil will constrict to pilocarpine 1%, because its receptors are free. One drop separates a scary neuro cause from a smudge of scopolamine on the finger.

05 Glaucoma, the same levers on pressure

Pressure is a balance: aqueous made by the ciliary epithelium versus aqueous drained. Every glaucoma drug does one or the other. This is your cross-link into Ocular Disease.

Make less aqueous

Turn down production

  • Beta-blockers (timolol), block β2 on the ciliary epithelium
  • α2 agonists (brimonidine), also add a bit of outflow
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide topical, acetazolamide oral)
Drain more aqueous

Open the outflow

  • Prostaglandin analogs (latanoprost), uveoscleral outflow, once at night, first-line
  • Pilocarpine, ciliary contraction opens the trabecular meshwork
Two safety facts the board loves

Timolol is out in asthma or COPD (β2) and in bradycardia or heart block (β1); betaxolol is β1-selective and safer for the lungs. Latanoprost quietly darkens the iris, grows the lashes, and can hollow the orbit (periorbital fat atrophy).

07 Rebuild it on paper

Reading this is not studying it. Close the card and do these blind. The pull from memory is the part that sticks.

1 · Draw the wiring

Sketch the eye. Label sphincter, dilator, ciliary muscle, and next to each write the branch (para/symp), the receptor, and the effect. Three clean rounds from memory.

2 · Fill the four-lever grid

Blank columns: lever, receptor action, pupil, accommodation, IOP, example. Fill it with nothing in front of you, then check against section 2.

3 · Duration ladder from zero

List the five cycloplegics longest to shortest with a concentration and one use each. Then flip and rank them by cycloplegic strength.

4 · Pupil-test decision tree

Write the branch: fixed dilated pupil → pilo 1% test; small pupil with ptosis → cocaine / apraclonidine, then hydroxyamphetamine; dilated with tonic near → dilute pilo. Give the reasoning, not just the drug.

5 · Teach it

Explain to an imaginary classmate why phenylephrine dilates without losing focus, and why atropine loses both. If you can teach it, you own it.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Drop these into your FoqusLab review queue and hit them on the 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Iris sphincter: branch, receptor, effect? Parasympathetic, M3, miosis.
  2. Iris dilator: branch, receptor, effect? Sympathetic, α1, mydriasis.
  3. Ciliary muscle contraction does which two things? Accommodation (slackens zonules) and opens the trabecular meshwork (lowers IOP).
  4. Strongest cycloplegic vs best pure mydriatic? Atropine (strongest cycloplegic); tropicamide (best mydriatic, weak cycloplegia).
  5. Which drug dilates without cycloplegia, and why? Phenylephrine; it is α1 on the dilator, not antimuscarinic on the ciliary body.
  6. Cycloplegics longest to shortest? Atropine > scopolamine > homatropine > cyclopentolate > tropicamide.
  7. Adie's tonic pupil confirming test? Constricts to dilute pilocarpine 0.125% (denervation supersensitivity); a normal pupil does not.
  8. Horner's pupil and the cocaine test? It fails to dilate (no noradrenaline release to potentiate).
  9. Which drop localizes Horner's, and how? Hydroxyamphetamine; dilates if the postganglionic neuron is intact, fails if the lesion is postganglionic.
  10. Atropinic blown pupil vs third-nerve palsy? Atropinic pupil will not constrict to pilocarpine 1% (receptors blocked); a CN3-palsy pupil will.
  11. Timolol contraindications, and the safer swap? Asthma/COPD (β2) and bradycardia/heart block (β1); betaxolol is β1-selective, safer for lungs.
  12. Glaucoma drugs: which cut production vs raise outflow? Production: beta-blockers, α2 agonists, CAIs. Outflow: prostaglandins (uveoscleral), pilocarpine (trabecular).
  13. Cataract surgery: pupil before extraction vs after IOL? Before: dilate (tropicamide, mydriatic). After: constrict (carbachol, miotic) to hold the implant.
