Glaucoma drugs: one tank, less in or more out

Pressure is a water level. The ciliary body fills the tank, two drains empty it, and every glaucoma drug does exactly one thing: turn the tap down or open a drain. This is the same production-and-outflow wiring from Card 1, now with the whole drug list hung on it.

01 The tank

IOP is production minus outflow. Aqueous flows out two ways: the trabecular drain (the main one, into Schlemm's canal) and the uveoscleral drain. Lower the level by making less or draining more.

Hook

Prostaglandins take the side drain (uveoscleral). Pilocarpine and netarsudil open the main drain (trabecular). The production reducers never touch a drain at all. Sort any drug by asking, tap or drain.

04 First-line, combos, and the crisis

First-line

A prostaglandin analog is first-line: the biggest single-drug drop (about 25 to 33 percent), once nightly, and no systemic effects. Beta-blockers are the classic alternative or add-on. When one drop is not enough, stack classes or use a fixed combo (timolol + dorzolamide, timolol + brimonidine, brinzolamide + brimonidine, netarsudil + latanoprost).

Acute angle-closure: order matters

An emergency. Lower the pressure fast first: topical timolol, brimonidine or apraclonidine, and dorzolamide, plus oral or IV acetazolamide, and IV mannitol if very high. Then pilocarpine to pull the peripheral iris out of the angle, but it only works once IOP drops below roughly 40 to 50, because a very high pressure leaves the iris sphincter ischemic and unresponsive. Definitive fix: laser peripheral iridotomy.

Hyperosmotics

Mannitol IV and oral glycerin or isosorbide pull water out of the vitreous for a fast, temporary drop in a crisis. Powerful but short-lived, and glycerin raises blood sugar (caution in diabetics).

05 The side-effect landmines

The board rarely asks the mechanism. It asks the signature harm. Learn the drug-to-trap pairs cold.

06 Rebuild it on paper

1 · Draw the tank

One inflow, two drains. Tag each drug class at its valve, and label which drain is trabecular and which is uveoscleral.

2 · Sort all six classes

Split them into make-less and drain-more without looking, then note which alpha-2 quirk lets it sit in both.

3 · Match the landmines

Blank the second column of the side-effect table and pair each drug with its signature harm.

4 · Angle-closure sequence

5 · Teach first-line

Give the three reasons a prostaglandin is first-line, and when you would reach for a beta-blocker instead.

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. IOP in one equation? Production (ciliary body) minus outflow (trabecular + uveoscleral).
  2. First-line drug, why, and dosing? Prostaglandin analog; biggest single-drug drop, no systemic effects, once nightly.
  3. Which classes reduce production? Beta-blockers, alpha-2 agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
  4. Which increase outflow, and by which route? Prostaglandins (uveoscleral); cholinergics and rho-kinase inhibitors (trabecular).
  5. Beta-blocker contraindications and safer swap? Asthma/COPD and bradycardia/heart block; betaxolol (beta-1 selective) is safer for lungs.
  6. Brimonidine red-flag population? Infants under 2 (apnea/CNS depression); also allergic conjunctivitis.
  7. CAI systemic side effects? Sulfa cross-reaction, paresthesias, kidney stones, metabolic acidosis.
  8. Prostaglandin local side effects? Iris darkening, lash growth, periorbital fat atrophy (PAP), hyperemia.
  9. Netarsudil signature side effect? Cornea verticillata (corneal whorls) plus conjunctival hyperemia.
  10. Pilocarpine risks? Retinal detachment, induced myopia, brow ache, dim vision from miosis.
  11. Acute angle-closure: first moves before pilocarpine, and why? Lower IOP first (timolol, brimonidine, acetazolamide, mannitol); pilocarpine works only once IOP is below about 40 to 50, since a high-pressure iris sphincter is ischemic.
  12. Definitive treatment for angle-closure? Laser peripheral iridotomy.
