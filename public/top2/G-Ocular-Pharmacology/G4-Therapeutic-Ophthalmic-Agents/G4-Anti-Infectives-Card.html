Anti-infectives: match the bug, respect the steroid

Every anti-infective decision is two questions. Which organism is this, and does it need a steroid held back? Get the red-eye triage right and the drug picks itself. Reach for a steroid on a red eye you have not identified and you can turn a dendrite into a disaster.

01 Red-eye triage: name the bug first

Before any drop, sort the infective red eye into five buckets. Each has one clue that gives it away.

Hook

The bug decides the drug. Allergic red eye (itch, stringy mucus, bilateral, no discharge) is the odd one out, it is not infective and gets antihistamine or mast-cell stabilizer, never an antibiotic.

03 Bacterial keratitis playbook

A corneal infiltrate with an overlying epithelial defect is keratitis until proven otherwise. Culture, then cover broadly and hit it hard.

Empiric cover

Central or severe ulcer: fortified cefazolin (gram-positive) plus fortified tobramycin or gentamicin (gram-negative), dosed hourly around the clock. Smaller or peripheral: a 4th-generation fluoroquinolone alone is usually enough. The logic is simple, cover both halves of the bacterial world until the culture narrows it.

The contact-lens bug

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the classic contact-lens keratitis organism: fast, a soupy hypopyon, and stromal melt within a day. Cover gram-negatives aggressively and never assume a red eye in a lens wearer is trivial.

04 Antivirals, and the steroid line

Herpes is where most people lose the point. The lesion tells you which arm of treatment is safe.

The steroid trap in HSV

Epithelial HSV (the dendrite) gets antiviral only. A steroid on it opens the dendrite into a geographic ulcer. Options: trifluridine 1%, ganciclovir gel 0.15%, or oral acyclovir/valacyclovir. Steroids are used in stromal or disciform HSV, where the problem is immune, but only under antiviral cover and with IOP watched.

Zoster (HZO) and adenovirus

Herpes zoster ophthalmicus: oral acyclovir/valacyclovir/famciclovir, ideally within 72 hours. Hutchinson's sign (a vesicle on the tip of the nose) marks nasociliary involvement and predicts the eye is at risk. Adenoviral conjunctivitis has no specific antiviral, treat supportively, warn about how contagious it is, and watch for subepithelial infiltrates and pseudomembranes.

05 Fungal and Acanthamoeba

Fungal keratitis

Suspect it after plant or vegetative trauma, or in a lens wearer, with feathery infiltrate margins, satellite lesions, and a slow course. Natamycin 5% is first-line for the filamentous fungi (Fusarium, Aspergillus). Voriconazole is a broad backup; amphotericin B covers yeasts like Candida. Steroids stay off.

Acanthamoeba (know this one cold)

Contact lens wearer, water exposure, and pain far out of proportion to what you see, a ring infiltrate, and radial keratoneuritis. Treat with a biguanide (PHMB or chlorhexidine) plus a diamidine (propamidine or hexamidine), for months. A ring infiltrate with hypopyon after extended lens wear is infectious keratitis, not a papillary reaction, do not soothe it and send it home.

06 The one rule that fails people

Never steroid an undiagnosed red eye

Steroids worsen HSV epithelial, fungal, and Acanthamoeba infections, they mask a worsening picture, they raise IOP in responders, and over time they grow cataract. If inflammation genuinely needs them (stromal HSV, severe iritis), give them with the correct anti-infective cover and monitor the pressure. When unsure of the cause, treat the infection and hold the steroid.

Ophthalmia neonatorum by day of onset

Day 1 chemical (silver nitrate). Day 2 to 5 gonococcal, sight-threatening, needs systemic ceftriaxone. Day 5 to 14 chlamydial, needs oral erythromycin (topical alone misses the lungs). Prophylaxis at birth is erythromycin ointment.

09 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Empiric cover for a severe central bacterial ulcer? Fortified cefazolin (gram-positive) plus fortified tobramycin or gentamicin (gram-negative); or a 4th-gen fluoroquinolone alone if less severe.
  2. Most common organism in contact-lens keratitis? Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
  3. Corneal side effect of ciprofloxacin? White crystalline corneal precipitate.
  4. HSV epithelial lesion and one antiviral? Dendrite with terminal end-bulbs; trifluridine 1% (or ganciclovir gel 0.15%, or oral acyclovir).
  5. Why no steroid on epithelial HSV, and when is it used? It opens the dendrite into a geographic ulcer; steroids only for stromal/disciform HSV under antiviral cover.
  6. Hutchinson's sign? Vesicle on the tip of the nose (nasociliary nerve); predicts ocular involvement in HZO.
  7. Adenoviral conjunctivitis treatment? Supportive; no specific antiviral; very contagious, watch for subepithelial infiltrates.
  8. First-line for filamentous fungal keratitis? Natamycin 5% (Fusarium, Aspergillus).
  9. Acanthamoeba: clues and drugs? Pain out of proportion, ring infiltrate, lens + water; biguanide (PHMB, chlorhexidine) plus diamidine (propamidine, hexamidine).
  10. Hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis under 24 h? Suspect gonococcal; systemic ceftriaxone plus saline lavage; emergency.
  11. Neonatal conjunctivitis: gonococcal vs chlamydial timing and treatment? Gonococcal day 2 to 5 (ceftriaxone); chlamydial day 5 to 14 (oral erythromycin).
  12. Four reasons a steroid is dangerous on an undiagnosed red eye? Worsens HSV/fungal/Acanthamoeba, masks infection, raises IOP, causes cataract.
