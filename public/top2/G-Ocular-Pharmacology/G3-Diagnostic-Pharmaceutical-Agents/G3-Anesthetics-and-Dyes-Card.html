Card05Diagnostic set

Anesthetics and dyes: numb it or stain it

These are the diagnostic agents, the DPAs. One family stops the nerve so you can touch the eye, the other lights up what is wrong on the surface. Small list, high yield, and one rule about anesthetics that the board asks every year.

01 Two jobs

Everything here does one of two things. An anesthetic silences the cornea so you can measure pressure, remove a foreign body, or examine a painful eye. A dye makes a defect or a sick surface visible. Both are diagnostic, held in the clinic, never sent home.

02 Topical anesthetics

They block the voltage-gated sodium channels in the corneal nerves, so the nerve cannot fire and the eye feels nothing. Onset is seconds, and the effect lasts about 15 to 20 minutes.

One thing to remember about the blink

An anesthetized cornea also loses its blink reflex and protective sensation. The eye is defenceless until the drop wears off, so the patient must not rub it, and you protect the surface until sensation returns.

03 The rule the board loves

Never dispense a topical anesthetic for home use

Repeated self-use is toxic to the epithelium: non-healing defects, a ring-shaped stromal infiltrate that mimics Acanthamoeba, corneal melt, even perforation and vision loss. On top of that it masks the very pathology that is getting worse. Anesthetics numb for an exam in the clinic, then stay in the clinic. If a stem hands a patient a bottle to take home for pain, that is the wrong answer.

04 Fluorescein: stains what is missing

Fluorescein is an orange dye that glows green under cobalt blue light. It does not stain intact epithelium. It pools in the tear film and floods any spot where the epithelium is gone, so a defect lights up bright green.

Seidel test

Put fluorescein over a suspected wound. A bright stream of diluted dye running out means aqueous is leaking, a full-thickness (penetrating) injury. This is the Seidel-positive picture that pairs with a capsular tear in trauma questions.

Two quick traps

Fluorescein stains soft contact lenses, remove them first (or use high-molecular-weight fluorexon). And its four everyday jobs beyond abrasions: applanation tonometry, tear break-up time, dendrite detection, and RGP fit patterns.

05 Rose bengal and lissamine: stain what is sick

These stain devitalized and degenerating cells and mucus, exactly the cells fluorescein ignores. That makes them the dry-eye stains, lighting up the exposed interpalpebral surface in keratoconjunctivitis sicca. They also mark an HSV dendrite well.

The one-line split

Fluorescein = cells GONE (an epithelial defect). Rose bengal and lissamine = cells SICK but still there (dry eye). Same eye, two questions. Rose bengal stings hard; lissamine green does the same job painlessly, so it is now preferred.

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Topical anesthetic mechanism? Block voltage-gated sodium channels, stopping nerve conduction.
  2. Workhorse topical anesthetic and why? Proparacaine 0.5%, least stinging.
  3. The one rule with topical anesthetics? Never dispense for home use; abuse causes non-healing defects, ring infiltrate mimicking Acanthamoeba, corneal melt, and masks disease.
  4. What reflex is lost after an anesthetic, and what do you do? The blink reflex; protect the eye and do not rub until sensation returns.
  5. What does fluorescein stain, and under what light? Epithelial defects and the tear film (not intact epithelium); glows green under cobalt blue.
  6. Seidel test positive means? A stream of diluted dye from a wound = aqueous leak = penetrating (full-thickness) injury.
  7. Fluorescein and a soft contact lens? It stains the lens; remove it first (or use fluorexon).
  8. What do rose bengal and lissamine green stain? Devitalized and degenerating cells and mucus (the dry-eye surface).
  9. Rose bengal vs lissamine green? Same targets; lissamine stings far less, so it is preferred.
  10. Fluorescein vs rose bengal in one line? Fluorescein = cells gone (defects); rose bengal/lissamine = cells sick but present (dry eye).
  11. Two everyday tests that use fluorescein? Goldmann applanation tonometry and tear break-up time.
  12. Are anesthetics and dyes DPAs or TPAs? Diagnostic agents (DPAs); the anti-infectives, anti-inflammatories, and glaucoma drugs are therapeutic (TPAs).
