Card03Color vision

Three cones, and the channels they feed

Color is three cone types, an opponent recode, and a naming system built on which cone is off. Learn the S/M/L peaks, the prot/deuter/trit rule, and the anomaloscope, then add the parallel pathways that carry color, motion, and the body clock.

01 Three cones, then an opponent recode

Normal color vision is trichromatic: three cone types, each tuned to a different band. The brain then recodes them into opponent pairs.

Two theories, both right, at different stages

Young-Helmholtz trichromatic theory describes the receptor stage (three cones). Hering opponent-process theory describes the neural stage: bipolar, ganglion, and LGB cells recode the cones into red-green, blue-yellow, and black-white opponents. That recode is why you never see reddish-green.

02 Naming a color defect

Two questions decide the name: how many cone types work, and which one is the problem.

Working conesClassMembers
3, one weak/shiftedAnomalous trichromacyProtanomaly (L weak), deuteranomaly (M weak), tritanomaly (S weak)
2 (one cone absent)DichromacyProtanopia (no L), deuteranopia (no M), tritanopia (no S)
1 cone onlyIncomplete monochromacyCone (e.g. blue-cone) monochromatism
0 cones (rods only)Complete monochromacyRod monochromatism (achromatopsia)
The prot / deuter / trit rule

Prot = first = L cone = red. Deuter = second = M cone = green. Trit = third = S cone = blue. So "-anomaly" = that cone is weak, "-anopia" = that cone is absent.

03 Frequencies and inheritance

Red-green is X-linked (the common one)

Protan and deutan defects are X-linked recessive, so about 8% of males and only ~0.5% of females. Deuteranomaly is the single most common color anomaly; deuteranopia is the most common dichromacy.

Blue-yellow is different

Tritan (S-cone) defects are rare and autosomal (not sex-linked), so equal in males and females. Acquired tritan defects also flag S-cone-vulnerable disease (glaucoma, some retinopathies).

Complete achromatopsia

Autosomal recessive, no working cones. Rods only, so poor acuity, nystagmus, photophobia, and no color. Its X-linked cousin, blue-cone monochromatism, affects mainly males.

05 The channels the cones feed

Ganglion cells sort into parallel streams to the LGB, plus a special one that never forms an image.

ChannelCarriesNotes
Magnocellular (M / Y cells)Motion, low contrastFast, large fields, high temporal, low spatial, color-blind
Parvocellular (P / X cells)Color (red-green), fine detailSlow, small fields, high spatial, high acuity
KoniocellularBlue-yellow (S-cone)Between the LGB layers
ipRGCs (melanopsin)Ambient light, non-imageTo the suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian) and the pupil reflex
Hook

Magno = Motion (big, fast, colorblind). Parvo = color and Particulars (small, slow, detailed). ipRGCs run the body clock, not what you see.

06 Cross-links

Say these out loud

Visual Biology: cone phototransduction and the S/M/L opsins live here. Clinical / Occupational optometry: the tests and the pass/fail standards are chair-side and job-screening tools. Ocular Disease: an acquired color defect (especially blue-yellow) is a red flag for optic nerve or retinal disease, unlike a stable inherited one.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. The three cone peaks? S about 420 nm (blue), M about 534 nm (green), L about 564 nm (red).
  2. Trichromatic vs opponent theory? Young-Helmholtz = receptor stage (3 cones); Hering opponent = neural recode into red-green, blue-yellow, black-white.
  3. Prot / deuter / trit map? Prot = L = red; deuter = M = green; trit = S = blue.
  4. -anomaly vs -anopia? -anomaly = that cone weak/shifted (anomalous trichromat); -anopia = that cone absent (dichromat).
  5. Most common color anomaly and most common dichromacy? Deuteranomaly (anomaly); deuteranopia (dichromacy).
  6. Two cone types dead means what? Only one cone works = incomplete monochromacy; zero cones = complete (rod) monochromacy.
  7. Inheritance of red-green vs blue-yellow? Red-green = X-linked recessive (about 8% of males); blue-yellow (tritan) = autosomal, rare, equal in sexes.
  8. Complete achromatopsia features and inheritance? Autosomal recessive, rods only: poor acuity, nystagmus, photophobia, no color.
  9. Screening test vs gold standard? Ishihara plates screen red-green; the anomaloscope (Rayleigh match) is the definitive/classifying test.
  10. Which common test misses tritan defects? Ishihara (it screens red-green only).
  11. Magnocellular vs parvocellular? Magno = motion, low contrast, fast, colorblind; parvo = color (red-green) and fine detail, high acuity.
  12. Where do ipRGCs (melanopsin) project? Suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian) and the pupillary light reflex, non-image-forming.
