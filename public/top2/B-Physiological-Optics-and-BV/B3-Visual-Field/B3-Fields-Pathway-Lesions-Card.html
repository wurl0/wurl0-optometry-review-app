Card02Fields & pathway

Where the field breaks names the lesion

The whole pathway is one rule: before the chiasm, a defect is one eye; at the chiasm, it splits the two temporal fields; behind the chiasm, it takes the same half of both. Learn the six cut points and the pupil test, and field questions become a lookup.

01 The pathway and its six cut points

Light from the right half of the world lands on the left half of each retina and travels to the left brain. Nasal fibers cross at the chiasm; temporal fibers stay put. Cut the line anywhere and the field drops out in a signature pattern.

03 Reading the pattern

Homonymous vs heteronymous

Homonymous = the same side of both fields (both right, or both left), so the lesion is behind the chiasm and on the opposite side of the brain. Heteronymous = opposite sides (bitemporal or binasal), which only the chiasm can produce.

Congruity localizes front-to-back

Congruity = how well the two eyes' defects match. The more congruous (matching), the more posterior the lesion. So an incongruous homonymous defect points to the optic tract; a congruous one points to the occipital cortex.

Two hooks

Temporal lobe = "pie in the sky" (Meyer's loop carries lower fibers, so it drops the upper field). Parietal lobe = "pie on the floor" (inferior quadrant). And macular sparing = occipital, because the occipital pole has a double blood supply.

04 The pupil: swinging flashlight

The light reflex splits off before the LGB, so it tests the afferent (input) nerve and the efferent (CN III output) separately. A retrochiasmal or LGB-and-beyond lesion leaves the pupil normal.

RAPD (relative afferent, Marcus Gunn)

Swing the light to the bad eye and both pupils dilate instead of constricting, because that eye sends a weaker signal. It is a sign of optic nerve or extensive retinal disease, and it is relative (a difference between the two sides).

Efferent (motor) defect

One pupil never constricts, to its own light or to the consensual light, because its CN III output is out. The other pupil reacts normally throughout. Afferent is an input problem, efferent is an output problem.

Wernicke's hemianopic pupil

Shine light on the blind hemiretina and the pupil does not react; shine it on the seeing half and it does. This localizes to the optic tract (the last place field and pupil fibers still travel together before the pupil fibers peel off).

05 Cross-links

Say these out loud

Visual Biology: the same pathway and the pupil light reflex arc, built up anatomically. Primary Eye Care: confrontation and perimetry map these defects at the chair, and the swinging-flashlight test is a two-second screen. Clinical / Neuro-optometry: pituitary tumors (bitemporal) and strokes (congruous homonymous with macular sparing) are the classic cases.

07 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. Optic nerve lesion defect? Monocular blindness of that eye, with a positive RAPD.
  2. Central chiasm lesion? Bitemporal hemianopsia (crossing nasal fibers), classically a pituitary tumor.
  3. Lateral chiasm lesions on both sides? Binasal hemianopsia (uncrossed temporal fibers).
  4. Homonymous vs heteronymous? Homonymous = same side of both fields = behind the chiasm; heteronymous = opposite sides = the chiasm.
  5. What does congruity tell you? The more congruous (matching) the defect, the more posterior the lesion (occipital); incongruous = optic tract.
  6. Pie in the sky? Superior homonymous quadrantanopsia from a temporal-lobe (Meyer's loop) lesion.
  7. Pie on the floor? Inferior homonymous quadrantanopsia from a parietal-lobe lesion.
  8. Occipital lesion hallmark? Congruous homonymous hemianopsia with macular sparing (dual blood supply).
  9. Right homonymous hemianopsia localizes where? A left-sided retrochiasmal lesion (left tract, radiation, or occipital).
  10. RAPD on the swinging-flashlight test? Swinging to the bad eye makes both pupils dilate; sign of optic nerve or severe retinal disease.
  11. Efferent pupil defect? One pupil never constricts to direct or consensual light (CN III output is out); the other reacts.
  12. Wernicke's hemianopic pupil localizes where? Optic tract; light on the blind hemiretina gives no reaction, light on the seeing half does.
