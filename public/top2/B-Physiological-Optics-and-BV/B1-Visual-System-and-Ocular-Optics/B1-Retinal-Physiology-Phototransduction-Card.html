Card05Retinal physiology

Light turns the receptor off

The photoreceptor is the one neuron that fires in the dark and quiets in the light. Learn the dark current, the cascade that shuts it, the visual cycle that reloads the pigment, and the adaptation curve, and the physiology questions collapse into one story.

01 Two receptors, split shift (duplicity)

Rods and cones divide the day. The duplicity theory says one system runs dim light, the other bright.

02 Phototransduction: light shuts the dark current

In the dark, the receptor is ON

Cyclic GMP holds the CNG sodium channels open, a steady dark current keeps the cell depolarized, and it releases glutamate tonically. Dark is the receptor's busy state.

Light runs the cascade and turns it OFF

A photon isomerizes 11-cis retinal to all-trans, activating opsin (metarhodopsin II). That activates transducin (a G protein), which switches on phosphodiesterase (PDE). PDE hydrolyzes cGMP, so cGMP falls, the Na channels close, the cell hyperpolarizes, and glutamate release drops.

The one-line chain

Light → retinal flips → opsin → transducin → PDE → cGMP down → Na channels close → hyperpolarize → less glutamate. The receptor is the neuron that quiets when lit.

03 The visual cycle (reloading the pigment)

All-trans back to 11-cis

Bleached all-trans retinal travels to the RPE, is reduced and then re-isomerized to 11-cis retinal (retinal isomerase), and returns to recombine with opsin. The building block is vitamin A (retinol), stored in the liver's Ito (stellate) cells.

Why vitamin A deficiency blinds you at night first

Rods run on the most pigment turnover, so low vitamin A starves rhodopsin and causes nyctalopia (night blindness) before day vision suffers.

04 Dark adaptation and the Purkinje shift

Step from sun into a dark room and sensitivity recovers in two stages: cones first and fast, then rods, slow and far deeper.

The Purkinje shift

As you go scotopic, peak sensitivity slides toward the blue (rods 507 vs cones 555). At dusk, reds go dark first and blues look relatively brighter. That shift is why the red bulb protects dark adaptation and why the ophthalmoscope's red-free light helps.

08 Spaced-repetition deck

Into the review queue, 1 / 2 / 3 / 7 / 14 day ladder. Answer out loud before you flip.

  1. The dark current? In the dark, high cGMP holds Na channels open, the cell is depolarized and releases glutamate tonically.
  2. What does light do to a photoreceptor? It hyperpolarizes the cell and reduces glutamate release (the receptor turns off).
  3. Role of PDE and cGMP? Light activates PDE, which hydrolyzes cGMP; cGMP falls, so the Na channels close.
  4. First molecular step of light capture? 11-cis retinal isomerizes to all-trans, activating opsin (metarhodopsin II).
  5. Cascade order after opsin? Opsin to transducin to PDE to cGMP down to channels close to hyperpolarize.
  6. Visual cycle and vitamin? All-trans retinal returns to the RPE and is re-isomerized to 11-cis; the building block is vitamin A (retinol), stored in liver Ito cells.
  7. Vitamin A deficiency first symptom? Nyctalopia (night blindness), because rods depend on the most pigment turnover.
  8. Scotopic vs photopic peaks? Rods 507 nm, cones 555 nm.
  9. Dark adaptation shape? Biphasic: fast cone branch, then the rod-cone break at ~7 to 10 min, then the slow, deeper rod branch.
  10. The Purkinje shift? In dim light peak sensitivity shifts toward blue (rods), so reds darken and blues look relatively brighter at dusk.
  11. ERG a-wave vs b-wave? a-wave = photoreceptors; b-wave = bipolar/Muller cells. Flat in retinitis pigmentosa.
  12. What do horizontal cells do? Lateral inhibition, sharpening edges and contrast.
