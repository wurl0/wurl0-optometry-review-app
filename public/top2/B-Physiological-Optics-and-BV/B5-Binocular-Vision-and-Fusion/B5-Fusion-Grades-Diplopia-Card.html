Card04Fusion & diplopia

One image from two: correspondence, fusion, diplopia

Card 1 moved the eyes together. This card is what the brain does with the two pictures: pair corresponding points, fuse what is close, see double what is not, and, when the eyes stop aligning, quietly adapt. Correspondence, the three grades, crossed vs uncrossed, and suppression vs ARC.

01 Corresponding points and the horopter

Two retinal points that share one visual direction are corresponding points. Stimulate a matched pair and you see one fused image; stimulate mismatched points and you see two.

Panum's fusional area

A thin zone straddling the horopter where slightly disparate images still fuse into one. Disparity within Panum is the raw material of stereopsis (depth); disparity beyond it is seen as physiologic diplopia.

02 The three grades of binocular vision

Worth ranked binocular vision in three ascending grades. Each grade assumes the one below it.

GradeNameWhat it means
ISimultaneous perceptionBoth foveas perceive at once (superimposition); tested with dissimilar targets
IIFusionBlend two similar images into one, and hold it. Tested with flat-fusion targets (near-identical, small control difference)
IIIStereopsisDepth from horizontal disparity within Panum. The highest grade
Fusion has two halves

Sensory fusion = the brain merges the two images. Motor fusion = the vergence effort (fusional reserves) that keeps them aligned so sensory fusion can happen. Lose motor fusion and the phoria breaks into a tropia.

03 Physiologic diplopia (the normal double)

It is normal, you just ignore it

Anything well off the horopter falls outside Panum and images double. Hold a finger up, fixate past it, and it doubles. The brain suppresses it, so you rarely notice.

Crossed vs uncrossed

Nearer than fixation = crossed (heteronymous) diplopia, prominent in exo deviations. Farther than fixation = uncrossed (homonymous) diplopia. Contrast with pathologic diplopia, the troublesome double vision of a manifest strabismus or a nerve palsy.

Hook

Near = crossed (the images swap sides, like crossing your arms to reach a close thing). Far = uncrossed.

04 When the eyes stop aligning: sensory adaptations

A constant manifest deviation threatens two miseries: diplopia (one object, two places) and confusion (two objects, one place). The young visual system adapts.

Suppression

Actively inhibit the deviating eye's image. It ends the diplopia, but a constantly suppressed eye in a child drifts into amblyopia.

Anomalous retinal correspondence (ARC)

The fovea of the fixing eye takes on a common visual direction with an extrafoveal point of the deviating eye, so the patient sees single despite the turn. It needs a long-standing, constant, unilateral deviation; alternating fixation prevents it.

Eccentric fixation and amblyopia

Eccentric fixation: the amblyopic eye fixes with a non-foveal point (a monocular sign). Amblyopia: reduced best-corrected acuity from strabismus, anisometropia, or deprivation, with a structurally normal eye.

05 The tests

Worth 4-dot (fusion vs suppression vs diplopia)

Red-green glasses over four lights (one red, two green, one white). 4 lights = fusion. 5 = diplopia. 2 red = OD suppression (left eye off), 3 green = OS suppression. Near tests peripheral fusion; distance tests central.

Correspondence and suppression

Bagolini striated glasses dissociate the least (most natural), good for suppression and ARC. The red-lens and afterimage tests also probe correspondence.

Stereoacuity

Titmus (contour, has some monocular cues; all nine circles = 40 seconds of arc), TNO and Randot (random-dot, remove monocular cues), Lang (no glasses). Lower arc-seconds = finer stereo.
